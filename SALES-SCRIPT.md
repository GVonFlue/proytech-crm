## How this call works

You are not selling a website. You are telling someone a website already exists
with their name on it, and asking if they will spend ten minutes looking at it.
That is a much easier sentence to say and a much easier sentence to hear — and
it is the only thing on this call that is genuinely unusual to the person
picking up.

- **One job: ten minutes on Logan's calendar.** You never demo it yourself, you
  never quote past the website price, you never negotiate.
- **Expect roughly thirteen dials per booked call.** The twelve nos are the cost
  of the thirteenth. That is the math working, not you failing.
- **You may give the build range: seven forty-nine to fifteen forty-nine**,
  depending on size and how much automation sits behind it. You never put a
  number on the monthly — that is quoted per client — and you never price the
  CRM or the automation packages, which are per person. Those are Logan's call.
- **"I don't know — Logan will" is a professional answer** and it has never lost
  anyone a deal. Guessing has.
- **If they ask to see it right there on the phone, the answer is yes.** Text
  Logan the business name and he will have it up in minutes. Never stall on that
  question — it is the most interested a prospect ever gets.

---

## 1. The opener

> "Hey — is this [First Name]? … [First Name], this is [Your Name] over at
> ProyTech, here in Wichita. This is going to be a slightly odd call. Can I have
> thirty seconds?"

Then stop talking. Let them say "sure." The pause is what makes the rest feel
like a conversation instead of a pitch.

---

## 2. The in

> "So — we build websites for [see the swap table below] around here. And rather
> than call and pitch you on it, we went ahead and built one for [Company Name].
> No obligation, nothing to sign, I'm not asking you to buy anything today. I
> just want to get it in front of you and let you look at it. Takes ten minutes."

Say "no obligation" once and mean it. Do not repeat it three times — repeating
it is what makes people think there is one.

Say it like it is already done, because it is. Do not soften it into "we could
build you one" — that is a different call and a much weaker one.

---

## 3. The question you will get almost every time

They say: *"Wait — you built me a website? Why would you do that?"*

> "Honestly? Because telling people about it doesn't work. Everybody's had
> somebody call them about a website. It's faster to just build the thing and
> let you look at it. If you like it, we'll talk. If you don't, you're out ten
> minutes and I'll leave you alone."

This is the most important answer in the whole script, because it is completely
true and they can hear that it is. Do not dress it up. Do not add a benefit. Say
it and stop.

## 4. One script, six industries — swap these words

Everything else on this page stays the same. Only these four things change based
on who picked up.

| Who you called | Say we build for | The line that lands | Hand to Logan + Garrett if |
|---|---|---|---|
| Real estate agent | agents around here | "…answers the person who fills out a form while you're in a showing." | Team lead, 25+ deals, or owns the brokerage |
| Lender | loan officers around here | "…catches the application that comes in at nine at night." | Runs a branch or has LOs under them |
| Roofing | roofers around here | "…answers the call you miss when you're on a roof." | More than two crews, or already running ads |
| HVAC / plumbing | HVAC companies around here | "…books the service call while you're under a house." | More than three trucks, or a dispatcher |
| Auto / PDR | shops around here | "…answers the estimate request while you're on a car." | Multiple bays or more than one location |
| Landscaping | landscaping companies around here | "…catches the quote request while you're on a mower." | Year-round crews, or commercial accounts |

The "line that lands" goes in whenever they ask what makes it different, or when
the call needs a nudge. It is specific to their day, which is why it works. Use
it once — it is a line, not a theme.

## 5. Find out which call you are booking

There are two kinds of prospect on the other end of this, and they get booked
differently. You need one question to tell them apart, and it sounds like small
talk.

> "Before I take up more of your time — how big is the operation these days? Is
> it mostly you, or do you have people?"

**Small operator — the usual case.**

- Book the ten minutes with Logan to walk through the demo. This is the standard
  path and most of your calls.

**Bigger operation — see the last column of the swap table.**

- Do not pitch the site as the whole thing, and do not talk price at all. Book a
  longer slot with Logan and Garrett.

> "Got it — then honestly the website's the small piece for someone your size.
> Let me get you thirty minutes with Logan and Garrett instead. They'll show you
> the site, but they'll also show you what runs behind it for an outfit with
> [crews / a team / multiple locations]. That's a better use of your time than
> me talking."

Flag it in the log as **HIGH VALUE** and text Logan the same day. Do not let it
sit in the sheet overnight.

---

## 6. Book it

> "Are mornings or afternoons better for you?"

They pick. Then narrow immediately — never offer an open calendar:

> "Perfect. Thursday at ten, or Thursday at two?"

Do not hang up without all of these. A booked call with a missing mobile number
is a no-show.

- Full name, spelled back to them
- Best email — read it back
- Best mobile — read it back
- Confirm: *"You'll get a calendar invite from Logan in the next few minutes. If
  it's not there in ten, check spam and text me."*
- Set the frame: *"He'll have yours up on the screen when you get on. Ten
  minutes, and there's nothing on the other side of it."*

### Five things to grab before you hang up

Logan tightens their site up in the half hour before the appointment, so what
they see is about their business and not a generic version of it. He can only do
that if you got these on the call. Ninety seconds, and it is the difference
between a good demo and a great one — ask it like you are being thorough,
because you are.

1. The business name exactly how they want it written
2. Their current website, if they have one
3. The three things they actually want more of — *"if you could pick what the
   phone rings for, what is it?"*
4. Where they work — town, radius, or the areas they cover
5. Where their photos live: *"Facebook? Google? He'll pull from there unless
   you'd rather send some over."*

That last question does double duty. It gets Logan what he needs, and it makes
the whole thing feel real to them before they ever get on the call — which is
most of why people actually show up.

## 7. The five you will hear most

### The catch — "Why would you build it for free?"

They say: *"Okay, what's the catch?"*

> "No catch. It's built, it's real, and looking at it costs you nothing. If you
> want it live, the build runs seven forty-nine to fifteen forty-nine depending
> on size and how much automation goes behind it, plus a monthly for hosting and
> keeping it running. If you don't want it, then we built a website for nothing.
> Happens more than you'd think."

This is now your most common objection, and it is a good sign — a person asking
about the catch is a person who is interested. Answer it flat and fast. Any
hedging here reads as a catch. The last sentence is the one that does the work,
so do not cut it.

### Price — "What's it cost?"

They say: *"What am I looking at for something like that?"*

> "Fair question. The build runs somewhere between seven forty-nine and fifteen
> forty-nine, depending on how big it is and how much automation you want behind
> it."
>
> "There's a monthly on top for hosting and keeping it running, but Logan sets
> that off what you actually need — I'd be guessing, and I'd rather not guess at
> your money. Same with the CRM side if you ever wanted it, that's priced per
> person. That's a big part of what the ten minutes is for."

Give the range immediately and without hedging. It screens out anyone who was
never going to spend it, and it stops them feeling like you dodged. Then stop
talking about money. Do not invent a monthly, do not price the CRM or the
automations, do not discount, do not hint at flexibility — you have no authority
to move any of it and everyone on the call knows it. If they say it is too much,
that is a fit problem, not a price problem, and Logan will handle it.

### Incumbent — "I already have a website"

They say: *"I've already got one."*

> "Sure — and that's kind of the point. You can put them side by side and just
> see. Ten minutes, and if yours is better, keep yours."

The demo does this work for you, which is the whole point — you are not arguing
about whether theirs is good, you are offering a comparison. Never criticise
their current site. If they tell you they paid someone for it in the last year,
that is a genuine disqualify: thank them, ask who built it, note the name, and
get off the phone cleanly. Selling over fresh money is how you get a reputation
in a small market.

### Brush-off — "Just text me the link"

They say: *"Just send it over and I'll take a look."*

> "Yeah, absolutely — what's the best number to send it to?"
>
> "And here's the thing — the next call is where we actually go through
> discovery. What you want it doing, what you want that phone ringing for.
> That's the part that makes it yours instead of just a page with your name on
> it. If I can get you booked this afternoon we can get this taken care of ASAP
> instead of dragging it out over two weeks."

If they still will not book:

> "No problem at all. Let me get Logan and Garrett to take it from here —
> they'll get it over to you and follow up directly."

Say yes instantly and never hedge — text Logan the business name and it goes out
the same day. Then take one swing at booking them today, because a link with
nobody on the other end converts badly: they open it for eight seconds while
doing something else and it dies. If they still only want it sent, hand it off
and stop working it — flag it **SEND ONLY** in the log and text Logan and
Garrett the name, number and email the same day. It is not your call to chase
from there, and letting go of it cleanly is worth more than a second attempt.

### Hard no — "Not interested"

They say: *"Not interested, thanks."*

> "Totally fair — I called you out of nowhere. Only thing I'd say is it's
> already built, so there's nothing to get out of. If you ever want to see it,
> I'm [Your Name] at ProyTech. Take care."

One attempt, said lightly, then let them go. If they say no twice, thank them
and hang up — genuinely. A setter who pushes past a second no costs us far more
in a market this size than the deal was worth. Log it as a no and move to the
next number.

## 8. Voicemail

First attempt only. Never on the second or third — that turns you into a
telemarketer in their contacts. Under fifteen seconds. Say the number twice,
slowly.

> "[First Name], it's [Your Name] with ProyTech here in Wichita. Odd reason for
> the call — we built a website for [Company] and I wanted to see if you'd take
> a look at it. Nothing to sign. [Number]. Again, [Number]. Thanks."

---

## 9. Things you cannot say

- **Do not send a link yourself.** Text Logan the business name and he sends
  it — that way what lands in their inbox is the version he wants them looking
  at.
- **Any number outside the seven forty-nine to fifteen forty-nine build range.**
  Never a monthly figure, never a CRM or automation price, never a discount,
  never "I might be able to do something" — including when they are about to say
  yes.
- **That the website is free**, or a gift, or that we are giving it away.
  Looking is free. The site is not.
- **Any promise about results** — traffic, leads, calls, rankings, "you'll be
  number one on Google," "guaranteed." None of it, not even as a joke. Search
  promises in particular are how agencies get sued.
- **A timeline or a launch date.** You do not know, and being wrong creates a
  problem Logan has to clean up on a live call.
- **A client's name.** "A top-ten agent here in Wichita" is fine. The name is
  not yours to give out.
- **Anything about how it is built**, what it runs on, or which vendors are
  involved. "That's Logan's side of the house" covers all of it.
- **Anything about another business's numbers**, volume or pricing — including a
  client's. Wichita is small and it gets back around.
- **Never imply you are an employee of ProyTech.** You are an independent
  representative. If asked directly, say exactly that.

---

## 10. Log it before the next dial

Every dial gets a row, including the no-answers. Two minutes of logging at the
end of a day is a whole day of guessing. When a week goes badly, the log is the
only way to tell whether it was the list, the opener, or the offer.

- Business name, contact name, number, industry
- Outcome: no answer / voicemail / not a fit / follow-up / booked / HIGH VALUE
- Their reaction to the demo line, in their words
- Any objection you hit, and what you said back
- Next action and date

Questions during a calling block go to Logan by text. Do not sit stuck for an
hour — ask, and keep dialing while you wait.
